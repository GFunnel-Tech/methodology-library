# ED-05 — Niche and offer build (niche audit → positioning line → three-tier offer sheet)

`source: stated` (S-13, sections "Welcome" onboarding sheet and "Crafting Your Offer 101";
S-14, the "Crafting Your Offer 101" videos, lessons 32–42) unless marked. Where the videos add
to or differ from the written documents, the item says so and cites S-14.

## Purpose
Choose a market that can pay and can be reached, say in one line what you do for it, and
turn that into a priced, three-option offer before any marketing is switched on.

## When to use
At onboarding, before ED-02 or ED-04; again whenever close rates or prices stall.

## Inputs
The owner's current offer and price, a 3-month and a 12-month goal (the onboarding sheet
asks for both), and candidate niches.

## Steps (his order)
0. **Commit to one offer before testing it** (S-14 · lesson 32 "What To Expect With Offers"):
   build an offer from your own skill (you do not have to run ads or make short clips if that
   is not what you can deliver), and do not switch offers at the first objections. His view:
   every offer meets objections, so changing it resets you to zero; the question is how well
   you are working it, not whether it is the wrong offer. Use the course's example sheets for
   ideas, never as a copy.
1. **Pick a high-margin market** (S-14 · lesson 33 "What Types Of Niches Should You Target"):
   avoid low-margin buyers (very small shops, brand-new businesses, cafés, restaurants) because
   they cannot fund acquisition or content; target businesses that sell something at a high
   ticket (home services, law, medical, roofing, solar, other service firms). His test: ask
   "if I got you one more client, would this project pay for itself?" — a yes means the price
   is easy to justify. He argues the market matters more than sales skill.
   **Market screen, video version** (S-14 · lesson 34 "How To Find Your Market & Method"):
   *pain* (how much it hurts sets how much you can charge), *purchasing power* (balance it
   against pain — the most pained are not always the ones who can pay), *easy to target*
   (findable on social platforms, in groups and networking circles), and *normal or growing*
   (a shrinking market is a headwind no offer overcomes). He sorts outcomes into **health,
   wealth, relationships** and says wealth outcomes (commercial work) carry the highest prices.
   **Niche in market, method, or both**: "video" → "video for one market" → "a named method
   for one market"; each step up the specificity ladder unlocks a higher price band (figures
   below).
1a. **Niche audit — four yes/no tests** (S-13 · "The Niche Checklist/Audit"): — four yes/no tests** (S-13 · "The Niche Checklist/Audit"):
   1. They are easy to find, online or locally.
   2. You can get a meeting with the decision-maker to *diagnose*, not a quote-and-bid process.
   3. The market has money and room to grow.
   4. Your skill (content, video, strategy) solves a problem they have — stay in your lane.
   A niche must pass all of them. (His document says "all 3" but lists four; we read four.)
   The video of the same audit (S-14 · lesson 35 "The Niche Checklist/Audit") teaches **three**
   tests — findable, has money and growth, you can solve its problem — and drops the
   "diagnose-not-quote meeting" test; the market lesson (lesson 34) uses yet another four
   (pain, purchasing power, findable, growing). `[tag: conflict]` We keep the union: findable,
   can pay, growing, pain you can solve, and access to a decision-maker for a diagnosis.
2. **Raise perceived value** (S-14 · lesson 36 "Offer 101 - The Value Equation"): he teaches
   the value equation from Alex Hormozi's *$100M Offers* (he credits it): value rises with the
   dream outcome and the buyer's *perceived* likelihood of getting it, and falls with time to
   result and the effort the buyer must spend. Practical levers: do the work for them
   (done-for-you beats handing over gear), deliver faster than the market, and raise perceived
   likelihood with case studies and testimonials — which is why proof-gathering comes first
   (ED-06).
2a. **Positioning line** — one sentence: *who* the market is, the *outcome* they get, the
   *old method* they drop, the *new method* you use. A coach approves it before moving on.
3. **Offer sheet** (S-13 · "Crafting Your Offer 101", template + worked example; S-14 ·
   lesson 40 "Crafting Your Offer 101", lesson 37 "Pricing Retainers Overview"):
   - **options, never one price or a custom quote** — one price invites yes/no, options turn the
     question into "which one?"; custom quoting is slow and does not scale (lesson 37);
   - start from *who* and *what outcome*, then the deliverables that reach it, split into
     **one-time** deliverables and **sticky** (monthly recurring) deliverables — without sticky
     ones there is no reason to keep paying, so the retainer does not hold (lesson 37);
   - the high tier "gives the farm away", the mid tier carries part of it, the base tier only
     what a base outcome needs (lesson 40);
   - three packages that grow by **term and depth** (template: 3, 6, 12 months);
   - a promise (the outcome);
   - a **guarantee** that reverses the client's risk;
   - **contingencies** — what the client must do for the guarantee to hold;
   - two **incentives**: one for paying in full, one for signing the same day, each a cheap
     extra that feels valuable.
3a. **Margin check** (S-14 · lesson 41 "Margins: What to shoot for"): cost every deliverable in
   every tier as if you did none of the work yourself (every shooter, editor and producer day
   rate); total delivery cost should stay at or under 30% of the price (a ~70% margin)
   `[tag: assumption] stated by S-14`. Over that, change the package before selling it.
3b. **Polished sheet** (S-14 · lesson 42 "How To Create Polished Offer Sheet"): a clean
   one-page visual of the tiers, **full prices only** (no monthly split), shown at the end of
   the sales meeting; he builds it from a free pricing template in a design tool.
3c. **Present it** (S-14 · lesson 37): name the tiers longest first and say which one "most
   clients" pick (the middle); do not show monthly figures. If asked to split payments, answer
   with a question — whether they need it, noting most clients pay in full — so the deal is not
   killed by a flat no. Setting a pay-in-full norm this way is a persuasion move; our version
   must be true (most clients really do pay in full) — see open edges.
4. **Contract** — a fixed term (3/6/12 months) on a monthly fee, then month to month (see
   "Contract terms" below).

## Decision points
- A niche failing any test → choose another before building the offer.
- Guarantee only alongside contingencies that the client controls.
- **Which guarantee** (S-14 · lesson 38 "Guarantees Explained"; the purpose is risk reversal for
  a buyer who does not know you yet). Four types, with his trade-offs:
  - *unconditional* (money back for any reason) — easiest to sell, highest risk, no commitment
    asked of the client;
  - *conditional* (we help you reach X; if not, you get Y, provided you did A, B, C) — **his
    preference**: it asks commitment, sets terms, and "future-paces" the win; harder to sell;
  - *anti-guarantee* (all sales final) — protects the business most, hardest to sell; framed as
    "our clients don't need one";
  - *implied / performance* (paid only on results, revenue share) — easy to sell, but only safe
    if you control or can see the whole sales process.
  Conditional variants he uses: keep working free until the outcome; extra months free if a
  stated metric does not rise; a **"love it or leave it"** first period (pay the first period,
  walk away after it with no further obligation, no refund of that period) when the main
  guarantee is not enough; and a **delayed second payment** until the first deliverable lands.
  Whichever you pick, own it and keep it. Warning he gives: a buyer who buys *only* because
  of the guarantee is usually a poor client.
- Contingencies protect the company: list what the client must do so that failing is nearly
  impossible (supply information, publish the content, grant access); a complaint then becomes
  a conversation about whether they did those things (S-14 · lesson 40).

## Outputs
A niche, a positioning line, an offer sheet with three tiers, and an agreement.

## Contract terms (described, not reproduced; S-13 · retainer agreement and client agreement)
Both templates cover: a fixed initial term, then month to month with 30 days' written
notice; no refunds; no chargebacks (raise disputes directly); a guarantee and its
contingencies as blanks; two revisions per round; a liability cap; confidentiality; the
company may end the agreement if the client ignores its advice.
**Conflict:** the retainer template keeps content ownership with the company unless the
client buys it out; the client agreement gives the client the deliverables, with a right
for the agency to reuse footage in its own marketing. `[tag: conflict]` (H-14)

## Open edges
- "Work for free until results" is the example guarantee. It needs a written results
  definition and a time limit to be safe (H-15).
- No retainer floor or upfront percentage is stated anywhere except "first month upfront"
  in one agreement (H-8).
- The niche screen exists in three versions (four written tests, three in the audit video,
  a different four in the market video) `[tag: conflict]`; ask which he uses now.
- "Most clients pick the middle" and "most clients pay in full" are said to steer the buyer.
  We may say them only when our own data shows them true; otherwise they are misleading
  sales claims (consumer-protection risk in any market).
- The 30% cost cap is not tested against real delivery costs (H-proposed: delivery margin).
- "Love it or leave it" and "delayed second payment" change the contract; they must be
  written into the agreement, not offered verbally, or they conflict with the fixed-term,
  no-refund templates above.

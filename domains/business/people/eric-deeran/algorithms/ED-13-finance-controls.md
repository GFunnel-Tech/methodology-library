# ED-13 — Finance controls: getting paid, the P&L ratios, payroll, tax and savings

`source: stated` (S-13, section "7 Figure Operations"; S-14, sections "7 Figure Operations"
and "Business Fundamentals") unless marked.

## Purpose
Collect money without chasing it, see each month's profit against target ratios, pay the
team on a fixed cycle with a check before money moves, and keep tax and personal savings
from being spent on the business.

## When to use
Steps 0–1 from the first client; steps 2–5 monthly once there is a team to pay.

## Steps
0. **Set up the money rails** (S-14 · lesson 27 "Setting Up LLC and Business Bank Account";
   lesson 28 "How You Should Process Payments (Merchant Account & Payment Processor)"):
   register the company with the state yourself rather than through a filing service, get a
   federal tax ID, then open a business bank account with both documents. For card payments he
   prefers a **merchant account from your own bank** plus the processor it pairs with, because
   an all-in-one processor can freeze or close an account after a dispute, while a bank you
   already hold an account with is more likely to back you and may charge less. He says he is not
   a tax or legal adviser.
1. **Put retainers on auto-pay** (S-14 · lesson 25 "How To Put Clients On Auto-Pay"): send the
   first month as an invoice so the card is saved; then create a subscription for the retainer
   amount, starting one month after the first payment, ending after the remaining months of the
   term (a six-month retainer = first payment + five scheduled cycles). The point is never chasing
   a client for a monthly payment. He uses an all-in-one processor here `[tag: conflict]` with
   step 0's advice to avoid one.
2. **Fill the P&L** (S-13 · "How To Use A P&L & Template"; S-14 · lesson 282 "How To Use A P&L &
   Template"): one sheet per month, copied from the last, finished **2–3 days after month end**
   `[tag: assumption] stated by S-14`. Income lines — revenue contracted (the full deal value),
   cash collected, refunds, chargebacks, financed revenue, total income; expenses in three
   buckets — **acquisition** (ad spend, referral payouts, paid shout-outs, recruiting ads),
   **labour** (sales, fulfilment, marketing), **other** (bank and card fees, software, insurance,
   office, travel, training…); then net income and tax to set aside. Small expenses come from the
   accounting software's own report, which pulls card and bank transactions automatically. Each
   line also as % of income.
3. **Check the ratios** against his targets (below), and **decide from the sheet, not from how
   the month felt** (lesson 282):
   - acquisition high → look at ad efficiency, new creative, and the no-show rate (booked calls
     that never show push the cost of each sale up);
   - labour high → is pay within industry ranges for each role?;
   - other high → what is being bought that does not need to be?
4. **Run payroll twice a month** (S-13 · "How To Track Payroll + Payroll Periods"; S-14 ·
   lessons 283–284): a payroll service holds each person's bank details and tax forms and files
   payroll taxes; a separate sheet is kept as the owner's own record. Periods: 25th–9th paid on the
   15th; 10th–24th paid at month end. One row per person: contractor or employee, payment method,
   base, commission, bonus, other, total. **An admin prepares, the owner types "approved", the
   admin enters it in the payroll service, then marks it "paid"** — two people, two checks, before
   money moves. Monthly payroll totals then split back into the P&L's sales and fulfilment labour.
5. **Set money aside before it is spent** (S-14 · lesson 282; lesson 31 "Setting Yourself Up For
   Success & Saving For The Future"):
   - tax: move **~30% of net profit** `[tag: assumption] stated by S-13 / S-14` to a separate
     account each month;
   - personal savings: save some share of every month **whatever the income** — the lesson's
     warning is the founder who promises to start saving at the next revenue milestone and never
     does, because every extra dollar becomes gear. Before buying gear, ask whether it will be
     used on paid client work or is just the newest model.

## His stated targets (`[tag: assumption] stated by S-13 / S-14`; falsifier: our own books)
| Ratio | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Cash collected ÷ revenue contracted | 60–70%+ |
| Acquisition ÷ income | ~30%, at most 35% |
| Labour ÷ income | 25–30% |
| Other ÷ income | 5–10% |
| Bank and card fees | ~2% of cash collected |
| Net margin | 30–50% |
| Tax set aside | ~30% of net |

## His savings illustration
$400 a month at an assumed 7% yearly return for 40 years ≈ $1.05M; $1,000 a month ≈ $2.6M.
The arithmetic is `[tag: forced]` (standard future-value of a monthly deposit); the 7% return
is `[tag: assumption] stated by S-14` and he says it is not financial advice. Falsifier:
actual long-run returns of the chosen fund, net of fees and inflation (the figures are
nominal, before tax).

## Business insurance (S-14 · lessons 29–30)
General and professional liability at about $1M cover, with customer-property protection,
on an annual policy (he quotes ~$71/month or ~$700/year); separate equipment insurance valued
at **today's replacement cost**, not purchase price, with theft cover (~$450/year at a $20K
example). All prices `[tag: assumption] stated by S-14` — one provider, one state, one date.
Clients on larger sets may ask for proof of cover. Detail in ED-22.

## Decision points
- A bucket above its range → that cost is the first to cut. `source: derived`
- A dispute-prone payment method → move card volume to a bank merchant account (step 0).

  order (delivery first, sales last), which explains why sales labour is largest at scale
  (H-9 narrows).
- The processor conflict (steps 0 and 1) is unresolved: which he uses for subscriptions now.
- The payroll example records an "independent contractor" on a fixed monthly base; see ED-12's
  classification note (proposed H-17).
- Tax at 30% of net is a placeholder; the right figure depends on entity, state and income.
- Entity choice (LLC vs. other forms) and country differences are not covered; his steps are
  for a US single-state company.
